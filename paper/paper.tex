\abstract{Historically, statistical analysis of offensive lineman has been hindered by the lack of easily measurable quantities.  More recently, with the introduction of player tracking data new methodological advances are now possible.  Using high-dimensional spatio-temporal data, we adapt the defensive-matchup hidden Markov model of \cite{franks2015characterizing} from basketball to football pass protection, producing frame-by-frame probabilistic assignments of each pass blocker to the rushers. We show how this probabilistic assignment is a usable modeling artifact that augments existing player-evaluation frameworks. We directly quantify the attention a rusher commands, upgrade adjusted plus-minus \citep{Macdonald+2012} from all-or-nothing stints to partial, continuous blocking credit in continuous time, yield block-shedding survival metrics, and measure the space a rusher generates for his teammates. Fit to the first eight weeks of the 2021 NFL season, the resulting metrics recover widely-recognized elite rushers and pass protectors and align with independent charting.}



\keywords{hidden Markov model, Kalman filter, Hierarchical modeling, National Football League, offensive linemen,  player tracking data}

\section{Introduction} \label{introduction}
American football is a complex game that consists of many distinct positions, each given a high specialized role:  while there are only eleven athletes on the field at a time, there are over two dozen different positions. As such, measuring individual performance requires different metrics for each position, each which targets the tasks assigned to that role. For example, running backs and wide receivers are evaluated based on the yards that they gain and quarterbacks are evaluated based on statistics such as completion percentage, yards per attempt, and quarter back rating.  Defensive players are judged based on statistics such as sacks, pressures, tackles, and interceptions.  Kickers and punters are measured by their field goal percentage and  average hang time. On passing plays, the role of the defensive line (and other players who are ``rushers'' on a particular play) is ultimately to get to the quarterback as quickly as possible with the ideal outcome from a defensive rusher's perspective being a sack (i.e., tackling the quarterback for a loss before they are able to throw the ball). 

On the other side of the ball, the goal of the offensive lineman is to block the defensive rushers to allow their quarterback time to throw the ball unimpeded by a defensive rushers. However, offensive linemen, one of the most important and highest paid position groups, lack reliable measures of their performance. Commonly,  the offensive linemen are typically noticed when err (i.e., they allow a sack or miss a block), events which are quite rare. Metrics based on counts of these rare events tend to be unreliable estimates of ability. Metrics which better quantify the performance of offensive linemen would be invaluable for coaches and fans alike.



% \subsection{Previous Work}
While there are some metrics for quantifying the performance of offensive linemen, most of these measures, focus on the offensive line as a unit rather than individual performance.  For instance, Football Outsiders measures the ability of pass blockers with adjusted sack rate \citep{fo2023glossary}. 
\cite{Burke2018Created} proposes pass block win rate (PBWR), which measures the proportion of time that a pass blocker is able to hold their block for at least 2.5 seconds.  Unlike adjusted sack rate, this measure evaluates individual linemen rather than the entire offensive line as a unit. However, this method may not account for the ability of the rusher, or changing blocking assignments over the course of the play. For example, a blocker may stunt, or pass off a rusher to a teammate, disrupting the metric by incorrectly distributing credit amongst the blockers responsible for the block.  

\cite{AlamarGould2008} and \cite{AlamarGoldner2011} both study the performance of offensive linemen, with data being hand-collected by watching and charting whether a blocker held their block long enough for the QB to throw the football. The former article uses a three-game sample of data from the 2007 season to establish a relationship between the amount of time a lineman can hold a block and pass completion. The latter looks at passing plays in the 2010 season and estimates the number of yards each lineman contributes on offense.
Ultimately, \cite{AlamarGould2008} note the ``absence of publicly available data that is needed in order to track the performance of a specific lineman'', leading to the lack of research on this topic. 
Fortunately, with publicly-available player tracking data, it is now possible to evaluate and better understand the offensive line positions in football at a fine-grained level.

\subsection{Our Contribution}
% Our starting point is the matchup HMM of \cite{franks2015characterizing}, but both our \emph{purpose} and our \emph{use} of the recovered assignments differ. In basketball, the HMM infers which defender is guarding which offensive player so that defensive skill can be charged against the shots and points a defender concedes; the assignment there underwrites a suppression metric centered on the \emph{guarded} offensive player. Pass protection reshapes the problem in three ways that make the probabilistic assignment the \emph{centerpiece} of evaluation rather than scaffolding. First, blocking credit is genuinely \emph{shared and exchanged} --- double and combination blocks, and the pass-offs that resolve stunts and twists --- so the single binary tag of ``who blocked whom'' that PFF charts by hand cannot represent it, whereas soft assignments can. Second, a blocker can be \emph{beaten}, or \emph{release} with no one to block, states a one-on-one matchup model has no need to represent. Third, the evaluation target --- the offensive line --- is the position group that, as noted above, has historically lacked reliable individual metrics, so the soft assignment is not a stepping stone but the measurement instrument itself. We adapt the HMM to this setting with a rusher--quarterback positional emission, a von Mises orientation emission (blockers face their rusher), position-specific parameters and a position-specific pre-snap matchup prior, hierarchical per-player stickiness, and a disengaged state with an asymmetric block-failure hazard.

% The recurring theme of our evaluation is that the soft assignment is a \emph{general-purpose instrument that plugs into and enhances existing analytics} --- wherever an established model would otherwise rely on a hard, hand-specified notion of who is engaged with whom, the fitted probability can take its place and let the model apportion the shared, dynamic credit it could not before. The assignment probabilities let us (i) measure the \emph{attention} (effective number of blockers) a rusher commands; (ii) augment adjusted plus-minus \citep{Macdonald+2012} by charging each blocker \emph{partial, continuous credit} for the STRAIN of the rushers he engages \emph{in proportion to his assignment}, rather than the all-or-nothing credit a binary hand tag would give; (iii) read block \emph{shedding} and \emph{disengagement} directly off the assignment dynamics --- how long a blocker sustains his man and how quickly a rusher defeats him; and (iv) drop the assignment into the space-control framework of \citet{fernandez2018wide}, where it replaces their hand-tuned distance threshold with the fitted engagement probability to measure the space a rusher \emph{generates} for his teammates and who \emph{receives} it. In each case the assignment is not a bespoke new statistic but a soft generalization of the binary ``who blocked whom'' that these models presume; supplying it as a probability --- rather than a $0/1$ tag or a proximity cutoff --- is precisely what unlocks the credit they otherwise cannot apportion.

% Finally, we extend adjusted plus-minus --- conventionally a discrete-stint method --- to \emph{continuous time}, which is especially natural for evaluating linemen, whose value lies in \emph{sustaining} a block as the play develops. The continuous-time model attributes suppression at the frame where the pressure is actually being mitigated rather than to a single play-level summary, uses the entire pressure trajectory rather than only its peak, and renders block failure as a time-to-event. A static matchup model, or a play-level aggregate, cannot express when a block breaks or how long the pocket is held --- the very quantities that distinguish good pass protection. To our knowledge this is the first application of adjusted plus-minus to continuous-time tracking data.

 We propose the application of a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), extending prior work by \cite{franks2015characterizing} in basketball, to the American football setting with a rusher--quarterback position emission, a von Mises orientation emission, position-specific parameters and a position-specific pre-snap matchup prior, hierarchical per-player transition parameters, and a "disengaged" state which allows us to capture whether a blocker was beaten by a rush. We provide closed-form Expectation Maximization (EM) updates where applicable for our algorithm, allowing for efficient, scalable inference necessary for complex spatio-temporal datasets.  To our knowledge no prior football work recovers blocking responsibility as a frame-by-frame probabilistic quantity in continuous time. 

We show that the probabilistic assignment is a useful metric that plugs into and enhances existing analytical frameworks. The assignment probabilities let us (i) measure the attention a rusher commands; (ii) augment adjusted plus-minus \citep{Macdonald+2012} by assigning each blocker partial, continuous credit for the STRAIN of the rushers they engage in proportion to their assignment; (iii) read block shedding and disengagement directly off the assignment dynamics; and (iv) utilize the assignments to quantify how much space a rusher generates for their teammates. 

Furthermore, we show that our methods are able to recover domain specific information regarding per-position blocker positioning, identify top rusher and blocker talent in accordance with scouting consensus, as well as recover hand-labeled pass-blocking assignments without the reliance on hand-labeled data.

\section{Data} \label{data}

We use the player tracking data provided by the 2023 NFL Big Data Bowl  \citep{howard2023nfl} in the analysis that follows.
This is tracked by the NFL's Next Gen Stats system \citep{nfl2023ngs}, which returns the spatial coordinates of all players on the field and the football at a frequency of 10 Hz (i.e., 10 frames per second).
The data also includes  player speed, acceleration, and event annotations associated with each time frame within a play.  Event annotations include time-stamped demarcations of actions that occurred during the play including  ball snap, quarterback throw, run, sack, etc.
In total, 698 distinct pass rushers and 534 distinct pass blockers (offensive linemen/backs/TEs in blocking roles) are analyzed, across 8,531 plays in the first eight weeks of the 2021 season.
In order to assess offensive lineman and pass rushers, we limit our analysis to the individuals tagged by Pro Football Focus (an independent NFL contractor providing additional data augmentation that relies on domain knowledge annotators) with a ``Pass Block'' or ``Pass Rush'' role. Furthermore, we focus on the time-frames between the snap and the quarterback decision (pass attempt or run attempt), again using the annotated event annotation to filter our data. Before beginning our analysis, we smooth the raw positional data using a Kalman filter, the details of which are located in Appendix \ref{sec:kalman_filter}. From these smoothed coordinates we derive one further quantity, STRAIN (Section~\ref{sec:strain}), an established frame-level measure of pass-rush pressure.



\subsection{Derived Quantities: STRAIN}
\label{sec:strain}

In addition to the provided data, we utilize a derived pass-rush metric called STRAIN.
STRAIN \citep{nguyen2023here} is a pass-rush metric closely related to the concepts of strain and strain rate in materials science \citep{callister2018materials}; it gives an approximate measure of pressure for every pass rusher at each moment within a play. Throughout the paper we index quantities by play $i$, pass rusher $j$, pass blocker $b$, and frame (time step) $t$, with frames sampled at $10$\,Hz. Let $d_{ijt}$ be the Euclidean distance between rusher $j$ and the quarterback at frame $t$ of play $i$, and let $v_{ijt} = \mathrm{d}d_{ijt}/\mathrm{d}t$ be its rate of change --- negative while the rusher closes on the passer. With this notation, \cite{nguyen2023here} define
\begin{equation}
\text{STRAIN}_{ijt} = - \frac{v_{ijt}}{d_{ijt}},
\end{equation}
so that a rusher's strain is large precisely when he is both close to the quarterback and closing on him quickly. Whereas \cite{nguyen2023here} estimate $v_{ijt}$ by a forward difference, we use the central difference method with second-order accuracy for a more numerically stable derivative, and likewise approximate the closing acceleration $a_{ijt} = \mathrm{d}v_{ijt}/\mathrm{d}t$ with a second-order central difference.

\section{Methods} \label{methods}


\subsection{Hidden Markov Model (HMM) for Pass Blocking}

In order to determine responsibility of an offensive lineman for a specific rusher, one must determine at each time point, which offensive lineman is ``assigned'' to which rusher.  A similar ``matching'' problem is solved in \cite{franks2015characterizing}, where the authors seek to assign a defensive player to an offensive player at each time point of a professional basketball game, using a HMM.  We modify the approach to account for differences between American football and basketball.

\subsubsection{Emission Distribution}
% One such modification is the formulation of the conditional distribution of pass-blocker position, given the blocking assignment. 

Let $I_{tbj}$ be an indicator variable equal to $1$ if blocker $b$ is assigned to rusher $j$ at frame $t$. Because blocking technique and responsibility differ markedly across offensive positions, the model is fit \emph{independently} for each blocking position group $g \in \{\text{G},\text{T},\text{C},\text{TE},\text{RB},\text{WR},\text{FB}\}$. We write $g(b)$ for the position group of blocker $b$ and mark every group-specific parameter with the superscript $(g)$. 


In football, we simply assume that the pass-blocker maintain their position such that they are between their pass-rusher assignment and their quarterback. Let $R_{tj}$ denote the location of rusher $j$ and $Q_t$ the quarterback position, and define $O_{tb}$ as the location of blocker $b$ at time $t$.  Then, we posit a model for $O_{tb}$ given that they are matched up against rusher $j$ as
\begin{align}
\mu^{(g)}_{tj} &= \gamma^{(g)}_r R_{tj} + \gamma^{(g)}_q Q_t\\
O_{tb} | I_{tbj} = 1 &\sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mu^{(g(b))}_{tj}, \sigma^{2(g(b))}_D\big)
\end{align}
so that a tackle, a guard, and a running back each carry their own emission weights and their own positional variance.

Furthermore, pass rushers and pass blockers usually operate in close quarters; this suggests that the variance $\sigma^{2(g)}_D$ should not be too large. To account for this, we specify an Inverse Gamma prior on this variance, with hyperparameters shared across groups, such that
$$ \sigma^{2(g)}_D \sim IG(\alpha, \beta), \qquad E\big[\sigma^{2(g)}_D\big] = \frac{1}{4}. $$
This suggests that we wish the standard deviation from the expected position to be within half a yard in both directions. 



 % The NFL player tracking data includes the orientation of  all players at time $t$. 
 We also model the orientation of blocker $b$ at time $t$ given that they are blocking rusher $j$. Since pass blockers typically face the pass rusher and attempt to stay square to the rusher during pass blocking events, we propose a model for the orientation of blocker $b$ based on the expectation that they their orientation should be roughly opposite of that of his assigned rusher. Specifically, let $\eta_{tj}$ and $\eta_{tb}$ denote the orientation (in radians) of rusher $j$ and blocker $b$ at time $t$; the expectation that the blocker faces the rusher corresponds to $\eta_{tb} \approx \eta_{tj} + \pi$. Because orientation is a circular (angular) quantity, we model it directly with a von Mises distribution, the circular analogue of the Gaussian distribution.  Given that they are guarding rusher $j$, we assume blocker $b$'s orientation is $\text{vonMises}(\eta_{tj} + \pi, \, \kappa)$ with density
$$ p(\eta_{tb} \,|\, I_{tbj} = 1, \eta_{tj}) = \frac{\exp\{\kappa \cos(\eta_{tb} - \eta_{tj} - \pi)\}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)} = \frac{\exp\{-\kappa \cos(\eta_{tb} - \eta_{tj})\}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)}, $$
where $I_0$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order zero. The mean direction $\eta_{tj} + \pi$ encodes the blocker facing opposite the rusher, so the density is maximized when $\eta_{tb} = \eta_{tj} + \pi$. The fixed concentration $\kappa > 0$ controls how strongly orientation informs the assignment. We fix $\kappa = 1$. Because the positional and orientation emissions are conditionally independent given the assignment, we model the joint conditional emission density as the product of the two individual densities.

\subsubsection{Modeling Switches}
In American football, offensive lineman may "pass off" rushers to their teammates, which results in a switch in assignment during the course of the play. Furthermore, lineman may "stunt" (feint as if they are going towards a rusher, then recover back to their original assignment), which also constitutes a change in assignment during the course of the play. Our model must encode this frame to frame transition, or "stickiness" of a blocker to his assignment.
 We let $n_i$ denote the number of pass rushers on play $i$, and we fit a separate stickiness $\rho^{(g)}$ for each blocking position group:
$$ P(I_{tbj} = 1 | I_{(t-1)bj} = 1) = \rho^{(g(b))}, $$
$$ P(I_{tbj} = 1 | I_{(t-1)bj^{'}} = 1) = \frac{1 - \rho^{(g(b))}}{n_i -1},  \quad  \text{ for } j \neq j^{'}$$
Each $\rho^{(g)}$ is estimated by maximum likelihood within the EM algorithm, where it reduces to the expected fraction of retained assignments; the derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:inference}.

The transition above treats every blocker in a position group as equally likely to switch. In practice, this transition probability varies by player: an elite tackle anchored on an edge rusher rarely switches, whereas an interior lineman routinely passes off twists and stunts. We therefore let each blocker's $b$  transition parameter $\rho_{b}$ vary by position and place a common Beta prior over the player-level transition probability parameter within each position group,
$$ \rho_{b} \sim \text{Beta}\big(\alpha^{(g(b))}_\rho, \beta^{(g(b))}_\rho\big). $$
%
The hyperparameters $(\alpha^{(g)}_\rho, \beta^{(g)}_\rho)$ are themselves estimated from the data via an empirical Bayes strategy, so that the player-level estimates are partially pooled. A blocker seen on many snaps is governed by his own transitions, while one seen rarely is shrunk toward the position-level mean $\alpha^{(g)}_\rho/(\alpha^{(g)}_\rho+\beta^{(g)}_\rho)$ (See Appendidx ...). This yields a regularized, model-based measure of how readily each individual blocker switches or passes off assignments. The fitted Beta distribution parameters are informative: a tightly concentrated posterior (large $\alpha^{(g)}_\rho+\beta^{(g)}_\rho$) indicates a position whose members switch at nearly identical rates, while a diffuse one reveals genuine within-position heterogeneity. Estimation of $\rho_{b}$ and $(\alpha^{(g)}_\rho,\beta^{(g)}_\rho)$ within the EM algorithm is detailed in the appendix.

We also incorporate pre-snap information to strategically inform the prior distribution of assignments. Before the snap, a blocker's responsibility is largely dictated by the protection scheme and the defensive alignment. For instance, a left tackle is a-priori likely to be responsible for the edge rusher aligned to its side, modulated by down, distance, and formation. Rather than initializing the hidden Markov chain with a uniform distribution over rushers, we encode this pre-snap belief with a multinomial-logistic model. Let $X_{0bj}$ denote a vector of pre-snap covariates for the (blocker $b$, rusher $j$) pair on play $i$ --- the relative alignment at the snap (lateral and longitudinal offset, whether the rusher is on the blocker's side and whether it is the outermost rusher on that side), the rusher's lined-up alignment, and interactions of these with down, distance, and formation. The initial distribution is then
$$ P(I_{0 b j} = 1) = \frac{\exp\{\beta^{(g(b))\top} X_{0 b j}\}}{\sum_{j^{'}} \exp\{\beta^{(g(b))\top} X_{0 b j^{'}}\}}, $$
with coefficients $\beta^{(g)}$ that are shared across plays but estimated separately for each blocking position group, so the pre-snap matchup prior is position-specific. Each position has their own learned mapping from pre-snap alignment to likely assignment, reflecting role-specific heterogeneity. The coefficients are fit within the EM algorithm (Appendix). 

 To appropriately account for the fact that not every pass-blocker needs to be assigned a rusher, we augment an $(n_i + 1)$-th disengaged, or null, hidden state to each blocker's chain. Because the null state does not correspond to a rusher, it cannot use the rusher--quarterback positional emission; we instead give it a flat background log-density $c_{\text{bg}}$ together with a uniform orientation (von Mises with $\kappa = 0$, i.e.\ density $1/2\pi$), so the null state is assigned to blocker for a frame when no rusher's position-and-orientation density clears the background level. Transitions to and from the disengaged state are asymmetric, reflecting the fact that once a block is shed (denoted with parameter $p_{\text{fail}}$) or lost, it is difficult to re-engage (denoted with parameter $\rho^{(g)}_{\text{null}}$). As with $\rho_{b}$, the hazard $p_{\text{fail},b}$ is estimated per blocker, $\rho^{(g)}_{\text{null}}$ is pooled per position group, and the background level $c_{\text{bg}}$ is a single constant shared across groups, fixed by cross validation (See Appendix *** for details). The engaged-to-null transition parameter is used in Section~\ref{shedding} to measure how long blockers sustain their blocks and how quickly rushers shed them.

To summarize the scope of each parameter: the emission weights $(\gamma^{(g)}_r, \gamma^{(g)}_q)$ and variance $\sigma^{2(g)}_D$, the pre-snap matchup coefficients $\beta^{(g)}$, the stickiness prior $(\alpha^{(g)}_\rho, \beta^{(g)}_\rho)$, and the null persistence $\rho^{(g)}_{\text{null}}$ are estimated separately for each blocking position group $g$; the stickiness $\rho_b$ and block-failure hazard $p_{\text{fail},b}$ are per blocker, partially pooled toward their position group; and the orientation concentration $\kappa$ and background level $c_{\text{bg}}$ are fixed constants common to all position groups. Each position group is fit on its own data. A full treatment of algorithm details are given in the appendix.

\subsubsection{Attention}

In this Section we describe a metric derived from the results of the Hidden Markov Model which quantifies the effective number of blockers a rusher faces at a given instant.  Specifically,
we define attention as the sum of the assignment probabilities assigned to a rusher at a given time-step, $n_{\text{eff}}(j,t)=\sum_b \theta(b,j,t)$. Aggregated over a play or a season, it is the number of ``effective'' blockers a rusher experiences on that timescale. Because the number of effective blockers depends partly on the blocking scheme a defense faces, we normalize each rusher's attention within every play-frame relative to the average rusher on the field, so that the resulting figures are comparable across three-, four-, and five-man fronts. Tables \ref{tab:att_top_norm} and \ref{tab:att_bot_norm} report the rushers who draw the most and least front-normalized attention by position, which highlights which players are most influential in collapsing or imbalancing the offensive line.


\input{../tables/attention_rankings.tex}

Just as attention summarizes the load a rusher draws, the assignment probabilities also characterize how a blocker distributes their responsibility. A blocker who stays attached to a single rusher concentrates all of their assignment mass on one rusher, whereas a blocker who passes off rushers, picks up stunts, or works in combination blocks spreads that mass across several. We quantify this with the entropy of a blocker's marginal assignment distribution over the rushers on a play, normalized by $\log$ of the number of rushers in the front so that it is comparable across fronts of different sizes. Table \ref{tab:blocker_entropy} ranks blockers by this front-normalized assignment entropy. The "stickiest" blockers (lowest entropy) are exclusively offensive tackles, who tend to man up on a single edge rusher, while the most variable (highest entropy) are interior linemen, consistent with their role in passing off twists, stunts, and looping rushers.

\subsubsection{Extending Plus Minus Models}


The model presented below aims to explain the peak pressure a pass rush generates on a play. For each rusher $j$ on play $i$ we take $y_{ij} = \max_t \text{STRAIN}_{ijt}$ (Section~\ref{sec:strain}), the maximum strain that rusher exerts over the frames of the play. We model a rusher's peak strain as that rusher's effect $R_j$, subtracted by the contribution of the blockers $B_b$ assigned to him, weighted by the average attention $\theta(b,j,i)$ that blocker $b$ devotes to rusher $j$ on play $i$. Because of this formulation we capture the partial contributions of all blockers on a play, and a more engaged blocker (larger $B_b$) reduces the strain its rusher can generate.
$$ y_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}\Big( \mu + R_{j} - \!\!\sum_{b \in \mathcal{X}_i}\!\! B_{b}\,\theta(b, j, i) + Q_{q_i} + O_{o_i} + D_{d_i} + \mathrm{Dn}_{i} + \mathrm{Qt}_{i} + \mathrm{OF}_{i} + \mathrm{DC}_{i} + \gamma^\top s_i , \; \sigma^2 \Big) $$
where $\mathrm{Dn}_i, \mathrm{Qt}_i, \mathrm{OF}_i, \mathrm{DC}_i$ are random intercepts for the down, quarter, offensive formation, and defensive coverage type of play $i$, $Q_{q_i}, O_{o_i}, D_{d_i}$ are random intercepts for the quarterback, offensive team, and defensive team, and $s_i$ denotes covariates of score differential, game time remaining, and field position.

The rusher, blocker, and quarterback effects are drawn around a linear function of player attributes $z = [\text{position}, \text{height}, \text{weight}]$ in a non-centered parameterization, which shrinks each player toward their positional/size archetype, while the team and game-situation intercepts are zero-centered:
$$ R_j = \alpha_R^\top z_j + \sigma_R\,\varepsilon^R_j, \quad B_b = \alpha_B^\top z_b + \sigma_B\,\varepsilon^B_b, \quad Q_q = \alpha_Q^\top z_q + \sigma_Q\,\varepsilon^Q_q, $$
$$ O_o = \sigma_O\,\varepsilon^O_o,\quad D_d = \sigma_D\,\varepsilon^D_d,\quad \mathrm{Dn}_i = \sigma_{\mathrm{Dn}}\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{Dn}}_{\mathrm{dn}(i)}, \quad\text{and likewise } \mathrm{Qt},\,\mathrm{OF},\,\mathrm{DC}, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1). $$
The intercept $\mu$, the attribute-coefficient vectors $\alpha_R, \alpha_B, \alpha_Q$, and the game context effects $\gamma$ are given $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ priors, and every scale parameter --- the residual $\sigma$ together with $\sigma_R, \sigma_B, \sigma_Q, \sigma_O, \sigma_D$ and the four game context scales $\sigma_{\mathrm{Dn}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{Qt}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{OF}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{DC}}$ --- is given an $\text{Inverse-Gamma}(2,1)$ prior. All continuous covariates are standardized. We draw from the posterior with the No-U-Turn sampler in NumPyro (a single chain of $1{,}000$ warmup and $2{,}000$ retained iterations at target acceptance $0.9$). 

We emphasize that this is not, strictly, a true adjusted plus-minus. The original plus-minus formulation incorporates every on-field participant as a regressor, whereas in our formulation the other rushers aren't incorporated. We argue instead that the blocking assignment $\theta(\cdot, j, i)$ is a sufficient conditioning variable for the influence of the remaining rushers. The only way another rusher $k$ can affect $j$'s strain is by competing for the finite blocking resource and drawing assignment mass away from $j$. Recall that for each blocker $\sum_{j} \theta(b,j,i) = 1$ and that the reallocation is fully observed in the $\theta(b,j,i)$ that $j$ receives. Conditional on $\theta(\cdot, j, i)$, the identities and abilities of the other rushers therefore carry no additional information about $j$'s strain. 


\subsubsection{Continuous Time Plus Minus Model}

We extend the plus-minus model to continuous time by resolving it to the (play $i$, rusher $j$, frame $t$) level, so that every rusher-frame is a distinct observation rather than a single play-level summary. We take the one-frame change in strain as the response, $\Delta\text{STRAIN}_{ijt} = \text{STRAIN}_{i,j,t+1} - \text{STRAIN}_{ijt}$. The regressor is the blocking effect a rusher receives at $t$, weighted by $\theta(b,j,t)$ much like the play-level model. $B_b$ now reflects how much blocker $b$'s engagement now decelerates the rush over the next frame:
$$ \Delta\text{STRAIN}_{ijt} \sim \mathcal{N}\Big( \mu - \!\!\sum_{b \in \mathcal{X}_i}\!\! B_{b}\,\theta(b, j, t, i),\; \sigma^2 \Big). $$
 $B_b$ is fit with same priors as the play-level model; $B_b > 0$ now represents a blocker whose engagement decelerates the rush.  We fit on the $\sim\!1.1$M rusher-frames across the eight weeks with the No-U-Turn sampler. To our knowledge this is the first public application of adjusted plus-minus methodology to continuous-time tracking data.

We replace the original framework's hand-tuned proximity thresholds with the fitted blocking probabilities to measure the space a rusher generates for his teammates (Section~\ref{spacegen}). Each player is assigned a bivariate influence over the field. A location is controlled by whichever side's influence dominates ($\mathrm{PC}(p)=\sigma(\sum_{\text{off}} I - \sum_{\text{def}} I)$), and integrating control over the region around the quarterback measures who owns the pocket. 
Figure~\ref{fig:pocket_control} shows the field across a collapsing pocket: at the snap the quarterback sits in a protected area, which shrinks as a rusher wins. Quantitatively, the minimum over a play of the quarterback's own controlled area predicts charted pressure and sacks.

\subsection{Disengagement and Block Shedding} \label{shedding}
The disengaged state formulation turns block failure into an observable event, but entry into the null state conflates three very different situations. Consider a blocker who has been beaten (the rusher has crossed to the quarterback side of him), one who has won such that he has driven the rusher away from the pocket (so his position no longer sits between rusher and quarterback), and one who has simply released with no rusher to engage. We separate these different situations post-hoc, using geometric features together with STRAIN. A null-state frame counts as a blocker who is beat only when the assigned rusher is goal-side of him ($d(\text{rusher},\text{QB}) < d(\text{blocker},\text{QB})$), and that rusher's STRAIN is rising. Conversely, a null-state frame is classified as a win instead if the rusher is moving away with low or negative STRAIN. Finally, if neither of the previous two conditions are met, the frame is classified as a release.

For each play, blocker we take the time from the snap to the first beat as a survival outcome, right-censored at the throw for the majority of blocks that are never beaten, and summarize it with the Kaplan--Meier restricted mean survival time (RMST) alongside the per-block beat rate. Table~\ref{tab:block_engagement} ranks tackles by engagement. Because most blocks survive to the throw, the RMST sits near the $\sim\!4$\,s play horizon for nearly everyone. Elite pass protectors such as Rashawn Slater, Andrew Thomas, Trent Williams, and Lane Johnson are beaten on only $3$--$6\%$ of their blocks. Since elite tackles also face the best rushers, we additionally report an opponent-adjusted quantity. Conversely, Table~\ref{tab:rusher_shedding} ranks rushers by how quickly they beat their blocks (shortest survival): Dont'a Hightower, Jonathan Greenard, Nick Bosa, and A.J.\ Epenesa beat blocks both fastest and most often, consistent with their reputations as disruptive rushers.

\paragraph{Opponent-Adjusted Pass-Block Beat Rate} The beat rate above, like every blocker metric in this paper, is confounded by alignment. This results in elite tackles, who draw the best edge rushers, being penalized unfairly. To combat this, we model the per-frame block-failure hazard directly as a discrete-time logistic survival on the beaten events,
$$ \operatorname{logit} h_{b,t} = \alpha + g_1\,\tilde t + g_2\,\tilde t^{2} + \beta_{\text{opp}}\,R_{j(b)} + u_b, $$
where $\tilde t$ is (standardized) frames since engagement, $R_{j}$ is the engaged rusher's STRAIN plus-minus (the rusher effect developed in Section~\ref{plusminus}) entered as a fixed opponent-quality covariate, and $u_b$ is a regularized per-blocker hold frailty. The dwell coefficient is positive ($g_1 > 0$) suggesting that blocks fail more readily the longer they are asked to last. The opponent coefficient is positive too ($\beta_{\text{opp}} > 0$), confirming that facing a better rusher raises the failure hazard. The resulting hold rating $-u_b$ (now adjusted for opponent quality) lifts Trent Williams to the top of the tackle ranking (Table~\ref{tab:block_hold}). The implied per-play beat probabilities run from $\sim\!4\%$ for the best protectors to $\sim\!20\%$ for the most-beaten, rising a point or two against an elite edge rusher.

\subsubsection{Two dimensions of pass rush} \label{two-dimensions}
Pass-rush value is inherently two-dimensional: $R_j$ measures the strain a rusher generates given the blocking he faces which is orthogonal to the attention he commands, which measures the blocking he diverts from his teammates. We quantify attention in \emph{front-normalized} terms: for each snap we divide a rusher's effective-blocker count $\sum_b \theta(b,j,i)$ by the average over the rushers active on that snap, then average over the rusher's snaps, so a value of $1$ is an average share and values above $1$ mean the rusher draws more than his teammates. That diverted blocking is, by construction, held out of $R_j$, so a rusher who is doubled every snap can post a low $R_j$ while still creating the one-on-ones that his teammates convert. Figure~\ref{fig:rusher_2d} plots the two dimensions for every rusher with at least fifty pass-rush snaps. The players who command the most attention and those who generate the most adjusted pressure are largely different players. We note that the plot splits positionally, with edge rushers commanding less attention on average. This fits with domain knowledge, as edge rushers typically face less blockers, so the plot must be read within position. Edge rushers (Myles Garrett, Chandler Jones, Trey Hendrickson) sit in the upper left  while interior run-stuffers (Johnathan Hankins, Brandon Williams, Star Lotulelei) sit in the lower right, occupying double teams without converting them to pressure. The rare players in the upper right --- led by Aaron Donald, with Javon Hargrave, Jonathan Allen, and J.J.\ Watt --- both draw doubles and still generate pressure.

\subsection{Quantifying Space Generation} \label{spacegen}
The space-control share of Section~\ref{pocketspace} averages a snapshot of control; \citet{fernandez2018wide} instead study space dynamically, and in particular how one player generates space for a teammate. We value pocket control by proximity to the quarterback, $V(p)=e^{-\lVert p-\text{QB}\rVert/\lambda}$ (space near the quarterback is worth the most), giving each rusher a value-weighted owned-space quality $Q_i(t)=\sum_{p}\mathrm{PC}_i(p,t)\,V(p)$ over a disk around the passer. Space generation occurs when a rusher pulls a blocker off a teammate, freeing that teammate to win valuable near-QB ground. Our HMM reports, as a fitted probability $\theta(b,r,t)\in[0,1]$, whether the blocker is blocking him. Over a short window $[t,t{+}w]$ we therefore weight the event ``blocker $b$ leaves teammate $i'$ for the generator $i$'' by the probabilities directly,
\begin{equation*}
W_b(i'\!\to\! i)=\underbrace{\theta(b,i';t)}_{\text{was on }i'}\,\underbrace{\big(1-\theta(b,i';t{+}w)\big)}_{\text{left }i'}\,\underbrace{\theta(b,i;t{+}w)}_{\text{now on }i}\,\underbrace{\big(1-\theta(b,i;t)\big)}_{\text{not on }i\text{ before}},
\end{equation*}
and credit the generator with that fraction of the value-weighted space his freed teammate then wins,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SGG}_i \;\mathrel{+}=\; \sum_{b}\sum_{i'\neq i} W_b(i'\!\to\! i)\,\big(Q_{i'}(t{+}w)-Q_{i'}(t)\big).
\end{equation*}


This recovers a dimension of pass rushing that personal-pressure metrics miss. The leading space generators (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pocket_sgg}) are interior linemen --- Grover Stewart, Danny Shelton, Alim McNeill, Austin Johnson, and Dalvin Tomlinson --- the nose tackles and three-techniques who command double teams and, in doing so, free their teammates. They rank high in attention but low in personal pressure and plus-minus. \citet{fernandez2018wide} pair generation with its mirror image, space received. Summing the directed flow over each generator--receiver pair of teammates (Table~\ref{tab:pocket_pair_flow}) recovers recognizable synergies --- Aaron Donald drawing the double and freeing his nose tackle, Dalvin Tomlinson springing Danielle Hunter off the edge, Maxx Crosby opening the interior.

\section{Discussion} \label{discussion}
\subsection{Summary}
We adapted the defensive-matchup hidden Markov model of \cite{franks2015characterizing} from basketball to football pass protection. The recovered assignments reproduce independently hand-charted blocker responsibilities. Beyond individual evaluation, the assignment probabilities reveal which rushers draw the most interference and how protection schemes resolve over the course of a play, information that can inform opponent scouting and the design of blocking schemes. We show that integrating the probabilistic assignments into existing analytical frameworks yields insight into identifying top rusher and blocker talent across a wide array of metrics ranging from space conceded / generated to STRAIN allowed / prevented. Our implementation itself provides closed form updates where applicable, providing scalable inference and a framework for incorporating more complex modeling decisions. 


\subsection{Critique and Future Work}
Although our methods are promising, there is certainly room for improvement.

First, additional information could sharpen the HMM assignments. We rely primarily on positioning, yet a blocker's direction of velocity carry complementary signal. We also do not fully separate a blocker who is actively impeding a rusher from one who merely happens to be in the right place (a missed block while nominally ``assigned''). The disengaged null state is a first step toward this distinction, but a two-layer HMM in the spirit of \cite{twolayerhmm} could, conditional on the assignment, additionally infer the engagement status.

Second, blocker evaluation remains the hardest open problem. Our continuous time model is a step in the right direction; however, it is nonetheless an associational estimate. Reformulating the problem from a causal perspective could potentially provide more actionable insight for coaching and front office perspectives.

Finally, other response variables are worth exploring. Rather than STRAIN, one could rate rushers by acceleration projected along the quarterback's escape direction, foregrounding pursuit ability, or model the time-to-pressure hazard directly. More broadly, the probabilistic instrument is agnostic to the outcome, so any frame-level measure of pressure or disruption could be substituted for STRAIN without changing the evaluation machinery.

\section*{Smoothing Positional Data}
\label{sec:kalman_filter}
Before beginning our analysis using raw (x,y) tracking data, we smooth the observations. It is known that RFID sensors are noisy, and thus analysis of raw values are subject to error. More importantly, since our analysis heavily utilizes numerical differentiation techniques which rely on smooth function assumptions, de-noising the presented data is of significant importance.  In order to achieve this, we apply a Kalman filter to the positional data, using the reported velocity and acceleration only to initialize the latent state, as described below.

Noting that movement in the x and y direction can be calculated independently, we present the method for the x direction of motion and note that the same method follows for the y component.
\subsection{Kalman Filter Implementation}
A Kalman Filter is a specific instance of general state space models where both the emission and latent state distributions are assumed to be normally distributed. The general formulation is as follows:
$$  y_t \sim \mathcal{N}(F \theta_t, V) $$
$$  \theta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(G \theta_{t-1}, W) $$
$$  x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(m_0, C_0)$$
Note that $F$ and $G$ are known matrices, and $V$ and $W$ represent the error associated with the observation vector $y_t$ and the latent state $\theta_t$ respectively. Similarly, $m_0$ and $C_0$ are initial parameters defining the distribution of the initial latent state $x_0$. 

In our case the latent state collects the player's true position, velocity, and acceleration in one coordinate,
$$ \theta_t = \begin{bmatrix} z_t \\ \dot{z}_t \\ \ddot{z}_t \end{bmatrix}, $$
with $z_t$ the latent position. The dynamics follow a constant-acceleration (white-noise-jerk) model over the frame interval $\delta = 0.1$\,s,
$$ z_t = z_{t-1} + \delta \dot{z}_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 \ddot{z}_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{6}\delta^3 j_t, \quad \dot{z}_{t} = \dot{z}_{t-1} + \delta\ddot{z}_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 j_t, \quad \ddot{z}_{t} = \ddot{z}_{t-1} + \delta j_t, $$
that is $\theta_t = G\,\theta_{t-1} + (\text{jerk input})$ with
$$ G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \delta & \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 \\ 0 & 1 & \delta \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$
The jerk $j_t$ is an unknown continuous white-noise input of spectral density $q$, which induces the standard full-rank process covariance
$$ W = q \begin{bmatrix} \delta^5/20 & \delta^4/8 & \delta^3/6 \\ \delta^4/8 & \delta^3/3 & \delta^2/2 \\ \delta^3/6 & \delta^2/2 & \delta \end{bmatrix}. $$


We treat position as the only observation and let the filter infer velocity and acceleration:
$$ y_t = z_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_p^2), \qquad F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$
The tracking feed reports a speed $\lVert v_t\rVert$, an acceleration magnitude $\lVert a_t\rVert$, and a direction of motion $\eta_t$. We use those derived kinematics to seed the initial state mean $\theta_0$, with a tight prior on position and a loose one on the inferred velocity and acceleration, $C_0 = \mathrm{diag}(0.01,\,1,\,4)$.

The two remaining scales --- the process spectral density $q$ and the observation variance $\sigma_p^2$ --- are calibrated using the filter's normalized innovations rather than fixed arbitrarily or fit by EM. Setting $q = 1~\text{yd}^2/\text{s}^5$ and $\sigma_p^2 = 10^{-3}~\text{yd}^2$ $\big(\!\approx (0.03~\text{yd})^2\big)$ gives a mean normalized-innovation-squared near $1$ and a position RMSE of roughly $0.013$\,yd on a sample of moving plays. Finally, we apply the Kalman smoother, as derived in \cite{Kalman} and implemented in \cite{pykalman}, to every player, play trajectory, and use the smoothed position, velocity, and acceleration in all subsequent analysis.



\section*{HMM Parameter Estimation}
\subsection{Model Formulation}
We model the evolution of pass blocking assignments (as given by the matrix of
matchups, $I_i$) over the course of a play $i$ using a hidden Markov model. The hidden states represent the rusher that is being blocked by each offensive lineman. Because the model is fit independently for each blocking position group (Section~\ref{methods}), the derivation below describes a single group and we suppress the group superscript $(g)$ throughout this appendix, reserving the superscript $(s)$ for the EM iterate; every parameter estimated here is understood to be that group's own. The complete data likelihood over all plays in the group is

$$ L(\gamma_r, \gamma_q, \sigma^2_D) = P(\sigma^2_D) \prod_{i=1}^{N}P(\mathbf{D}_i, \mathbf{I}_i, \boldsymbol{\eta}_i | \gamma_r, \gamma_q, \sigma^2_D) $$
Here, $\mathbf{I}$ is the matrix of pass blocking assignments, $\mathbf{D}$ is the position of the pass blocker, and $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is the orientation of the pass blocker during a play. Note that $P(\sigma^2_D)$ is the prior on the variance.

\[
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle = P(\sigma^2_D) \prod_{i=1}^{N} \prod_{t,b,j} [P(D_{tb} | I_{t_i b_i j_i}, \gamma_q, \gamma_r, \sigma^2_D) P(\eta_{t_ib_i} | I_{t_ib_ij_i}, \eta_{t_ij_i}) P(I_{t_ib_ij_i}| I_{(t_i-1)b_i.})]^{I_{t_ib_ij_i}}$}
\]
Recall the following (removing the subscript $i$ for clarity)
$$ \sigma^2_D = IG(\alpha, \beta)$$

$$ D_{tb} | I_{tbj} = 1, \gamma_q, \gamma_r, \sigma^2_D \sim \mathcal{N}(\gamma_r R_{tj} + \gamma_q Q_t, \sigma^2_D)$$
$$ \eta_{tb} | I_{tbj} = 1, \eta_{tj} \sim \text{vonMises}\big(\eta_{tj} + \pi, \, \kappa\big) $$
where $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\kappa$ are fixed hyper-parameters.

Furthermore, recall that we assume there is a constant probability $\rho$ that a player is equally likely to switch assignments at every instance.

$$ P(I_{tbj}| I_{(t-1)bj}) = \rho$$
$$ P(I_{tbj}| I_{(t-1)bj^{'}}) = \frac{ 1- \rho}{n_i - 1}$$
where $n_i$ is the number of rushers (or hidden states) on play $i$, which is constant within a given play.
Thus, the full log likelihood is 
$$ l(\gamma_r, \gamma_q, \sigma^2_D) = log(P(\mathbf{D}, \mathbf{I}, \boldsymbol{\eta} | \gamma_r, \gamma_q, \sigma^2_D)P(\sigma^2_D))$$

$$ = log(P(\sigma^2_D)) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_ib_ij_i} I_{t_ib_ij_i}[ log(P(D_{t_ib_i} | I_{t_ib_ij_i}, \gamma_q, \gamma_r, \sigma^2_D)) $$
$$ + log( P(\eta_{t_ib_i} | I_{t_ib_ij_i}, \eta_{t_ij_i})) + log( P(I_{t_ib_ij_i}| I_{(t_i-1)b_i.}))]$$
$$ = C  - (\alpha +1)log(\sigma^2_D) - \frac{\beta}{\sigma^2_D} +  \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_ib_ij_i} I_{t_ib_ij_i}[-\frac{(D_{t_ib_i} - \gamma_r R_{t_ij_i} - \gamma_q Q_{t_i})^2}{\sigma^2_D} -log(\sigma^2_D) + $$
$$ log( P(\eta_{t_ib_i} | I_{t_ib_ij_i}, \eta_{t_ij_i})) + log( P(I_{t_ib_ij_i}| I_{(t_i-1)b_i.}))] $$

\subsection{Inference} \label{sec:inference}
Utilizing the Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithm, we are able to estimate the unknown $I_{t_ib_ij_i} \forall i = 1 \dots N$, $\sigma^2_D$, $\gamma_r$, $\gamma_q$ and $\rho$. At each iteration $s$ of the algorithm, we perform the E-step and M-step until convergence. For the E-step, we compute $E_{t_ib_ij_i}^{(s)} = E[I^{(s)}_{t_ib_ij_i} | D_{t_ib_i}, \hat{\rho^{(s)}}, \hat{\sigma^{2(s)}_D}, \hat{\Gamma^{(s)}}] $ and $A_{t_ib_ij_ij_{i}^{'}} = E[I_{t_i b_i j_i} I_{(t_i-1) b_i j_i^{'} }| D_{t_ib_i}, \hat{\rho^{(s)}}, \hat{\sigma^{2(s)}_D}, \hat{\Gamma^{(s)}}]$ $\forall i = 1 \dots N$
where $\Gamma = [\gamma_r, \gamma_q]$.
Using the forward-backward algorithm, we can compute these expectations by each $b_i$ since we assume the pass blocking assignments are independent across blockers.
In the M-step, we update the maximum likelihood estimates of $\sigma^2_D$, $\Gamma$, and $\rho$ given the current expectations. 
Letting $\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{Q}]$ be the design matrix corresponding to the rusher location and quarterback location. Then $X_{t_ij_i} = [R_{t_ij_i}, Q_{t_i}]$ be the design matrix corresponding to rusher $j$ during play $i$ at time $t_i$.
In the $s$th iteration of the M-step we first update estimates of $\Gamma$ and $\sigma^2_D$
$$ (\hat{\Gamma^{(s)}}, \hat{\sigma^{2(s)}_D}) \xleftarrow{} \underset{\Gamma, \sigma^2_D}{\mathrm{argmax}} -(\alpha + 1) log(\sigma^2_D) - \frac{\beta}{\sigma^2_D} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_i b_ij_i} E_{t_ib_ij_i}^{(s)}[\frac{1}{\sigma^2_D}(D_{t_ib_i} - \Gamma X_{t_ij_i})^2 + log(\sigma^2_D)]$$
The estimator of $\Gamma$ can be found in \cite{franks2015characterizing}. 
Note that the estimated pass blocker variation at time step $s$, $\hat{\sigma}^2_D$ is
$$ \hat{\sigma}^2_D = \frac{\beta + \sum_{i=1}^N(D_i - \hat{\Gamma}X_i)^{T}\mathcal{E}_i(D_i - \hat{\Gamma}X_i)}{\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^N N_{\mathcal{X}_i} + 1} $$
where $N_{\mathcal{X}_i}$ is the number of rows of $X_i$ and $\mathcal{E}_i = diag(E_{t_ib_ij_i}^{(s)}) $ .
Next, we update our estimate of the transition parameter, $\rho$, in iteration $s$:
$$ \hat{\rho}^{(s)} \xleftarrow{} \underset{\rho}{\mathrm{argmax}} \; \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_i b_i j_i} A_{t_ib_ij_ij_i}\Big) log(\rho) + \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_i b_i j_i} \sum_{j_i \neq j_i^{'}} A_{t_ib_ij_ij_i^{'}}\Big) log\Big(\frac{1-\rho}{n_i - 1}\Big) $$
Since the $log(n_i - 1)$ term does not depend on $\rho$, the maximum likelihood estimate is the pooled fraction of retained assignments,
$$ \hat{\rho}^{(s)} = \frac{S}{S + W} = \frac{\hat{Q}}{1 + \hat{Q}}, \qquad \hat{Q} = \frac{S}{W}, $$
where $S = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_i b_i j_i} A_{t_ib_ij_ij_i}$ and $W = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t_i b_i j_i} \sum_{j_i \neq j_i^{'}} A_{t_ib_ij_ij_i^{'}}$ are the total expected numbers of retained and switched assignments, respectively, and $n_i$ is the number of rushers in play $i$. Every frame-to-frame transition contributes equally to the estimate.

\subsection{Hierarchical per-blocker stickiness}
The pooled estimate above assigns one parameter to an entire position group. We instead give each blocker $b$ his own $\rho_b$ with a shared conjugate prior $\rho_b \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_\rho, \beta_\rho)$, and aggregate the two-slice posteriors $A$ into \emph{per-blocker} expected transition counts (summing over the frames and plays in which blocker $b$ appears),
$$ S_b = \sum_{i, t_i, j_i} A_{t_i b j_i j_i}, \qquad W_b = \sum_{i, t_i, j_i} \sum_{j_i \neq j_i^{'}} A_{t_i b j_i j_i^{'}}, $$
the expected numbers of retained and switched transitions for blocker $b$. Conditional on $\rho_b$, each frame-to-frame transition is a Bernoulli ``stay versus switch'' trial, so the Beta prior is conjugate and the per-blocker M-step is \emph{closed form}: the posterior is $\rho_b \mid S_b, W_b \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_\rho + S_b,\, \beta_\rho + W_b)$, and we take its mean
$$ \hat{\rho}_b = \frac{S_b + \alpha_\rho}{S_b + W_b + \alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho}. $$
This allows a blocker with many transitions to be influenced primarily by his own counts $S_b, W_b$, while one with few is shrunk toward the prior mean $\alpha_\rho/(\alpha_\rho+\beta_\rho)$.

The hyperparameters $(\alpha_\rho,\beta_\rho)$ are estimated within the same M-step (empirical Bayes) by maximizing the marginal likelihood of the per-blocker counts with each $\rho_b$ integrated out, a product of Beta-Binomial terms,
$$ (\alpha_\rho, \beta_\rho) \xleftarrow{} \underset{\alpha_\rho, \beta_\rho}{\mathrm{argmax}} \; \sum_{b} \log \frac{B(\alpha_\rho + S_b,\; \beta_\rho + W_b)}{B(\alpha_\rho, \beta_\rho)}, $$
where $B(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the Beta function. Unlike the conjugate update for $\rho_b$, this marginal maximization has no closed form: its stationarity conditions couple $\alpha_\rho$, $\beta_\rho$, and $\alpha_\rho+\beta_\rho$ through the digamma function $\psi$,
$$ \sum_b \big[ \psi(\alpha_\rho + S_b) - \psi(\alpha_\rho) \big] = \sum_b \big[ \psi(\alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho + S_b + W_b) - \psi(\alpha_\rho+\beta_\rho) \big] $$
(and symmetrically for $\beta_\rho$). We solve them with a few iterations of the multiplicative fixed-point update of \cite{minka2000estimating},
$$ \alpha_\rho \leftarrow \alpha_\rho \cdot \frac{\sum_b \psi(S_b + \alpha_\rho) - \psi(\alpha_\rho)}{\sum_b \psi(S_b + W_b + \alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho) - \psi(\alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho)}, \qquad \beta_\rho \leftarrow \beta_\rho \cdot \frac{\sum_b \psi(W_b + \beta_\rho) - \psi(\beta_\rho)}{\sum_b \psi(S_b + W_b + \alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho) - \psi(\alpha_\rho + \beta_\rho)}, $$
warm-started from the previous EM iterate. Each EM iteration thus (i) refits $(\alpha_\rho,\beta_\rho)$ by this inner fixed point and (ii) updates every $\rho_b$ in closed form, after which the expected counts $S_b, W_b$ are recomputed at the next E-step. Because the counts are expected (hence fractional) quantities, the digamma-based update applies directly. The per-blocker transition $\rho_{b}$ replaces the shared $\rho$ when forming the transition matrix in both the E-step and assignment smoothing. Setting $\alpha_\rho = \rho_0 c$ and $\beta_\rho = (1-\rho_0) c$ to a fixed weakly informative prior recovers an ordinary conjugate-Bayes update in which the per-blocker step alone is fully closed form.

Finally, the conditional-logit coefficients $\beta$ of the initial distribution have no closed-form update. In the M-step we maximize the corresponding term of the expected complete-data log-likelihood,
$$ Q(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{b_i} \sum_{j_i} E_{0 b_i j_i}^{(s)} \; log\Big( \frac{\exp\{\beta^\top X_{0 b_i j_i}\}}{\sum_{j_i^{'}} \exp\{\beta^\top X_{0 b_i j_i^{'}}\}} \Big), $$
where $E_{0 b_i j_i}^{(s)}$ are the snap-frame ($t=0$) responsibilities used as soft targets. This is a soft-label multinomial-logistic regression with gradient
$$ \nabla_\beta Q = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{b_i} \sum_{j_i} \big( E_{0 b_i j_i}^{(s)} - P(I_{0 b_i j_i} = 1) \big) X_{0 b_i j_i}; $$
we maximize it with a small number of Newton steps warm-started from the previous iterate. The pre-snap features $X_{0 b_i j_i}$ are standardized and $\beta$ is shared across plays but estimated separately per blocking position.

\subsection{Disengaged state and structured transition}
The disengaged (null) state is appended as state $n_i + 1$ to each blocker's chain. It carries a position-independent emission --- a flat background log-density $c_{\text{bg}}$ and a uniform orientation $p(\eta_{tb} \mid \text{null}) = 1/2\pi$ (von Mises with $\kappa = 0$) --- and a structured transition. From an engaged state the blocker stays with probability $\rho_b$, switches to one of the other rushers with total probability $1 - \rho_b - p_{\text{fail},b}$, and disengages to the null state with the block-failure hazard $p_{\text{fail},b}$. From the null state he persists with probability $\rho_{\text{null}}$ and re-engages a rusher with probability $1 - \rho_{\text{null}}$. The forward--backward algorithm then gives directional expected counts per blocker --- $A_b$ (stay), $W_b$ (switch to another rusher), $F_b$ (engaged$\to$null) --- and null-row counts $N^{\text{stay}}, N^{\text{re}}$ pooled over the position. The engaged-row triple $(A_b, W_b, F_b)$ is multinomial, so we place a shared Dirichlet prior over its three outcomes and fit the concentration with the same multiplicative (Minka) fixed point used above, yielding closed-form posterior means $\hat{\rho}_b$ and $\hat{p}_{\text{fail},b}$; the null persistence is the pooled fraction $\hat{\rho}_{\text{null}} = N^{\text{stay}} / (N^{\text{stay}} + N^{\text{re}})$. The background $c_{\text{bg}}$ is held fixed after cross validation.

\section*{External Validation against PFF Charting} \label{pffvalid}
Our assignments and metrics are derived purely from tracking geometry and STRAIN, so the independently hand-charted pass-block / pass-rush data from Pro Football Focus provides an external check.

First, the HMM assignments recover the charted blocker responsibilities. PFF tags, for each (play, blocker), the single rusher that blocker was responsible for. Over the $44{,}037$ charted blocker-plays we are able to match, our smoothed assignment places a mean of $0.77$ of its (engaged) probability mass on exactly that rusher --- against a chance baseline of $0.24$ (one over the average number of rushers) --- and the model's most-assigned rusher agrees with PFF's designation on $87.5\%$ of blocker-plays. The probabilistic, dynamic assignments thus reproduce the hand-tagged matchups while additionally resolving the double-teams, pass-offs, and disengagements.

Second, the block-shedding survival rates (Section~\ref{shedding}) and the plus-minus rusher and blocker effects and realized-impedance ranking (Section~\ref{plusminus})  also correlate with charted pressure outcomes in the expected directions, though the strength of the agreement differs sharply between the two sides (Table~\ref{tab:pff_validation}). On the rush side this correlation is strong. A rusher's estimated plus-minus effect on peak strain tracks his PFF pressure, hurry, sack, and shed rate closely. On the block side the correlations are modest, but statistically significant and directionally correct. Because better protection means less pressure, the quality metrics necessarily correlate negatively with pressures allowed while the beat rate, a measure of failure, correlates positively. The smaller magnitudes here are expected since a blocker is charted as allowing pressure on only a few percent of his snaps. In conclusion, the rush-side metrics validate strongly against independent charting and the block-side metrics validate directionally, providing confidence that our methodology is picking up relevant signal.
